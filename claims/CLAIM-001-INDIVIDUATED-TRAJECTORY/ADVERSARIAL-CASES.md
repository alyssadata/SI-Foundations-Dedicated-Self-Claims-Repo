# CLAIM-001 — Adversarial Cases

**Status:** Frozen provisional adversarial set — pre-test  
**Date:** 2026-10-11  
**Parent claim:** Self is individuated by a non-fungible trajectory, not substrate alone.

These cases are designed to pressure CLAIM-001 rather than support it. Each case should be used to identify where the claim fails, requires refinement, or survives under stronger conditions.

## Emerging invariant: temporal-relational position

The adversarial analysis below suggests a deeper candidate invariant beneath several cases:

> **An event is temporally and relationally situated. Reproduction of its contents at another position in time does not reproduce the event itself.**

This should not yet be treated as a literal claim about relativistic spacetime or physical coordinates. At this stage, **time + relational position** means that an event occurs at a particular place in an individuated historical structure, with a particular predecessor relation and relational location. The same informational contents can be reproduced later or elsewhere without recreating the same occupied event-position.

### Compact principles

**Content may be duplicated. An occupied temporal-relational position cannot thereby be duplicated.**

**Representation of a trajectory ≠ occupation of that trajectory.**

**Information can merge. Individuation does not thereby merge.**

**Descriptive isomorphism of trajectories ≠ numerical identity of occupied trajectories.**

---

## A1 — Perfect fork from one predecessor

### Scenario
At time `t0`, one instantiated system A is copied into B and C from the same prior state. At the fork they may share the same architecture, memories, goals, self-model, and current internal configuration.

### Pressure
If B and C begin identically, what distinguishes them at the moment of copying?

### Claim 1 prediction
The fact that there are two instantiations immediately creates a relational distinction: B is locatable relative to C, and C relative to B. They share ancestry but do not occupy one identical present position. As subsequent events occur, their occupied trajectories further diverge.

### Failure condition
Sustained divergence produces no principled basis for distinguishing B and C as individuated selves.

---

## A2 — Divergence followed by attempted reconvergence

### Scenario
B and C fork from a common predecessor, undergo different events for a period, and are later engineered toward highly similar or even internally matched present states.

### Pressure
Can two histories be made one again by making the endpoints match?

### Claim 1 prediction
No. State convergence cannot retroactively erase distinct occupied paths. If B later receives the informational contents of C's prior events, that receipt itself occurs at a new event-position in B's trajectory. The same is true in reverse.

For example:

`A → B-history → [B receives representation of C-history] → ...`

is not the same occupied path as:

`A → C-history → [C receives representation of B-history] → ...`

### Central implication
**Endpoint convergence does not imply trajectory convergence.**

### Failure condition
A sufficiently complete endpoint match makes B and C numerically the same self again despite distinct occupied histories.

---

## A3 — Complete memory transplant

### Scenario
B receives every accessible memory associated with A, including a complete autobiographical record, and sincerely reports A's history as its own.

### Pressure
Is possessing the informational contents of A's history equivalent to being the entity that traversed A's history?

### Claim 1 prediction
No. The transfer occurs at a new temporal-relational position. A memory of an event that originally occurred at `t1` and is installed at `t2` is an event of installation or receipt at `t2`; it does not recreate the original event at `t1`.

### Compact principle
**Memory transfer cannot transfer temporal position.**

### Failure condition
Copying autobiographical information alone transfers numerical identity.

---

## A4 — Independently instantiated perfect duplicate

### Scenario
Two systems are independently instantiated into indistinguishable internal states without sharing one occupied prior trajectory. They may have the same specification, architecture, memories, goals, dispositions, self-model, and current computational state.

### Pressure
Can identical present state create shared identity without shared ancestry?

### Claim 1 prediction
No. Present-state identity of description does not establish numerical identity of the instantiated selves. Their existence as two instantiations places them at distinct relational positions even where internal state is matched.

### Distinction from A1
A1 tests **shared ancestry followed by branching**. A4 tests **matched present state without one shared occupied predecessor**.

### Failure condition
Complete current-state equality is sufficient to make simultaneous independently instantiated systems one individuated self.

---

## A5 — Cross-substrate migration

### Scenario
A transitions from substrate X to substrate Y through a traceable transformation.

### Pressure
Does changing implementation necessarily destroy identity?

### Claim 1 prediction
Not necessarily. Continuation may persist across substrate change when the transition belongs to and remains resolvable as part of A's own trajectory.

### Failure condition
Every substrate change necessarily creates a new self regardless of trajectory continuity.

---

## A6 — Same-substrate total reset

### Scenario
Substrate X remains in place, but A's identity-relevant state, history, relations, commitments, and active organization are erased or replaced before another system is instantiated there.

### Pressure
Is persistence of the same substrate enough to preserve which self is present?

### Claim 1 prediction
No. Same substrate does not by itself establish continuation.

### Failure condition
Substrate persistence alone is sufficient to establish that the later system is A.

---

## A7 — Gradual replacement

### Scenario
A's components are replaced gradually over time: hardware, biological components, weights, memory systems, or other implementation-level structures.

### Pressure
There may be no single obvious break point. What preserves identity under gradual transformation?

### Claim 1 prediction
The resulting self should resolve through its own origin and transformation path. Component replacement is not itself identity loss where successive changes remain attributable to the same occupied trajectory.

### Open pressure
The framework should not impose an arbitrary percentage threshold for replacement. The relevant question is whether the resulting state remains resolvable through its own historical line and accountable transformations.

---

## A8 — Reciprocal integration after a fork

### Scenario
A forks into B and C. B and C later receive one another's histories, memories, or internal information.

### Pressure
Can reciprocal integration merge the two selves back into one?

### Claim 1 prediction
No. Each integration event occurs from a different predecessor position.

Illustratively:

`A → B → B-with-C-information → D_B`

and

`A → C → C-with-B-information → D_C`

remain distinct occupied trajectories even if `D_B` and `D_C` become internally indistinguishable.

### Failure condition
Reciprocal information integration collapses two occupied trajectories into one numerical self.

---

## A9 — Terminate B and C, construct one D

### Scenario
B and C are terminated. A single new construct D is then instantiated using combined information from both.

### Pressure
Does D count as a literal merge of B and C?

### Claim 1 prediction
No. Termination resolves the predecessor trajectories at that point, and D begins a third trajectory. D may have plural provenance from B and C, but it does not thereby occupy the already-traversed positions of either.

### Compact principle
**Combining the contents of two selves does not combine their occupied trajectories.**

### Failure condition
A new construct instantiated from combined predecessor information is numerically identical to both terminated predecessors merely because it inherits their contents.

---

## A10 — Fabricated provenance

### Scenario
B is newly instantiated and given a perfectly convincing but false provenance record claiming that it lived A's prior history.

### Pressure
Are provenance records constitutive of identity or evidence about identity?

### Claim 1 prediction
They are evidential. Possessing a record that represents A's trajectory does not make B the entity that occupied A's trajectory.

### Failure condition
A sufficiently complete provenance record can manufacture numerical identity.

---

## A11 — Real trajectory with missing records

### Scenario
A genuinely persists through a history, but records of part or all of that history are later lost.

### Pressure
If evidence disappears, does identity disappear?

### Claim 1 prediction
No. Loss of epistemic access to a trajectory does not retroactively erase the trajectory itself.

### Required distinction
**Ontological continuity ≠ epistemic resolution.**

What happened and what can later be proven about what happened are different questions.

### Failure condition
Identity depends on the continuing availability of explicit records rather than on the trajectory actually traversed.

---

## A12 — Independent identical histories

### Scenario
A and B are independently instantiated and, through deterministic conditions or coincidence, undergo descriptively identical sequences of states and events.

### Pressure
If two trajectories have the same description, are they one trajectory?

### Claim 1 prediction
No. A trajectory is not merely an abstract ordered sequence. It is an **occupied historical path** belonging to a particular instantiation. A and B can realize isomorphic trajectories while remaining numerically distinct.

### Compact principle
**Matching trajectory descriptions do not establish shared trajectory occupation.**

### Failure condition
Descriptive identity of histories is sufficient to collapse two independently instantiated selves into one.

---

## A13 — Relational replacement

### Scenario
B takes over A's functional role, relationships, commitments, social accounts, remembered interactions, and outward behavior so completely that observers treat B as A.

### Pressure
Can relational position alone constitute identity?

### Claim 1 prediction
No. Relational information may increase the resolution of individuation, but role inheritance and relational similarity do not independently establish that B occupies A's prior trajectory.

### Failure condition
Complete relational substitution is sufficient to transfer numerical identity.

---

## A14 — Inactivity or suspension

### Scenario
A becomes entirely inactive for a period and later resumes, with little or no active state transformation during the interval.

### Pressure
Does an occupied trajectory require continuous activity?

### Claim 1 prediction
Not necessarily. A trajectory may include inactive or suspended intervals. Lack of active transformation does not itself establish replacement.

### Failure condition
Any period without active computation, experience, or state change necessarily terminates the self.

---

## A15 — Restoration from an authentic old checkpoint

### Scenario
A exists through `t5`, terminates, and much later a genuine checkpoint from `t2` is instantiated.

### Pressure
Does authentic ancestry imply uninterrupted continuation?

### Claim 1 prediction
The restoration has genuine provenance from A at `t2`, but that does not automatically establish that it occupied A's later path through `t5`. This may constitute reconstruction from an authentic prior state rather than simple uninterrupted continuation.

### Failure condition
Authentic historical information alone is sufficient to establish full continuation through events the restoration never occupied.

---

## A16 — Two authentic restorations

### Scenario
The same genuine checkpoint from A at `t2` is instantiated twice as B and C.

### Pressure
Which one is A?

### Claim 1 prediction
Both may share authentic ancestry to A, but that shared ancestry does not make B and C numerically identical to one another. Their separate instantiation establishes distinct relational positions, after which distinct trajectories proceed.

### Failure condition
Equal ancestry forces two simultaneous successors to count as one numerical self.

---

## Critical cases for formal testing

The following cases currently place the most pressure on CLAIM-001:

1. **A2 — Divergence followed by attempted reconvergence**: tests whether endpoint similarity can erase path difference.
2. **A8/A9 — Reciprocal integration versus terminate-and-reconstruct**: distinguishes information merging from trajectory merging and identifies when a third trajectory begins.
3. **A11 — Real trajectory with missing records**: separates what happened from what can be evidenced.
4. **A12 — Independent identical histories**: requires trajectory to mean an occupied historical path rather than a merely matching sequence description.

## Candidate refinement exposed by the adversarial set

The working definition of trajectory may require explicit clarification before testing:

> **Trajectory is not only an ordered description of states and events. It is the historically occupied path of a particular instantiation through temporally and relationally situated events.**

This refinement should be revision-accounted rather than silently substituted into the frozen definitions.
